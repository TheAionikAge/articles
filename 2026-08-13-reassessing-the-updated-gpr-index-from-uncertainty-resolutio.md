## The GPR Index Was Built to Capture Threats and Realizations, Not Uncertainty Resolution

When Caldara and Iacoviello introduced the Geopolitical Risk index, they made a deliberate design choice: the index tracks *events* — threats, acts, escalations — not the cognitive state of market participants. The methodology is transparent: automated text searches across major newspapers for terms signaling geopolitical tension, crisis, war, terrorism (Caldara & Iacoviello, 2022). A spike means something happened or was credibly threatened. It does not mean "uncertainty rose" in any abstract sense. It means a bomb went off, a leader issued an ultimatum, a troop movement crossed a border.

This distinction matters because the literature often conflates the two. Uncertainty is a latent variable — a fog. Events are observations — lightning in the fog. Caldara and Iacoviello's own 2018 working paper shows that *realized* adverse events (acts) and *unrealized* ones (threats) have different economic signatures. Threats, paradoxically, often exert stronger macroeconomic drag than acts — precisely because they leave the fog intact. Resolution arrives with the act; the threat *is* the unresolved tension (Caldara & Iacoviello, 2018). The index captures both, but it was never built to measure the dissipation of uncertainty. It measures the arrival of danger.

I have seen analysts treat a GPR spike as "uncertainty increased, so investment will pause." That reading mistakes the map for the territory. The index tells you a threat emerged or an act occurred. What happens to uncertainty afterward — whether it resolves, lingers, or metastasizes — is a separate question, one the index does not answer. The updated GPR series inherits this DNA. Its value lies in event chronology, not uncertainty accounting. To use it otherwise is to read the seismograph as a weather vane.

## Adverse Events Drive Economic Impact More Than Uncertainty Resolution

The asymmetry between threats and their realization is not a measurement artifact — it is the central empirical finding that shapes the updated GPR index's design. When a geopolitical threat materializes into an act, markets often experience a form of uncertainty resolution. The fog lifts; the range of outcomes narrows. Paradoxically, this resolution frequently produces *smaller* economic effects than the threat itself generated.

The research is clear on this point: "Realization of events often leads to uncertainty resolution with smaller economic effects, while threats have stronger impacts." This is not an edge case. It is the dominant pattern. A threat creates an open-ended distribution of catastrophic scenarios. Each market participant prices their own worst case. The aggregate effect is a broad, persistent drag on investment, hiring, and consumption. When the act occurs — a invasion launches, a sanction regime activates, a leadership transition completes — the distribution collapses. The worst cases either happen or vanish. The remaining uncertainty is narrower, more tractable. Economic agents adjust.

This asymmetry has direct implications for index construction. If the GPR index were primarily an uncertainty gauge, it should spike at threat emergence and decay upon realization. Instead, it captures both threats *and* acts as elevated risk readings — because both represent adverse events, and both carry economic weight. The index does not treat realization as "resolution" in the benign sense. It treats realization as the adverse event's second phase, often with distinct but still-significant economic consequences: supply chain rupture, financial contagion, commodity shock.

The updated index's emphasis on adverse events is therefore not a methodological choice in search of a justification. It is a response to the evidence. Threats hit harder. Acts persist longer. An index that conflated them with "uncertainty" — treating a resolved crisis the same as an open threat — would systematically misrepresent the economic signal policymakers and investors actually face. The GPR index avoids this error by design.

## The Updated GPR Series Shifts Focus to Event Measurement, Not Cognitive States

The evolution of the Geopolitical Risk (GPR) index reflects a deliberate methodological shift: newer iterations prioritize the measurement of adverse geopolitical events over the quantification of uncertainty resolution as a cognitive or market state. This alignment is not incidental but grounded in the empirical structure of the Caldara–Iacoviello framework, which the updated series extends. Rather than attempting to gauge the dissipation of ambiguity — a construct that is empirically elusive and context-dependent — the index operationalizes risk through normalized counts of geopolitical threats and their realization as discrete acts. These are observable occurrences: invasions, sanctions, terrorist attacks, leadership coups, or military escalations. Each is tallied according to predefined lexical and contextual criteria, then normalized against historical newspaper coverage to ensure cross-temporal comparability.

This event-centric design directly responds to research showing that both threats and acts carry measurable economic weight, even if their mechanisms differ. As established in prior analysis, threats often generate broader, more persistent drags on economic activity through heightened uncertainty and worst-case scenario pricing. Acts, while sometimes resolving specific uncertainties, frequently trigger distinct adverse outcomes — supply chain disruptions, financial contagion, or commodity shocks — that sustain or even amplify economic impact. Treating either as mere "uncertainty" would misrepresent their role as drivers of real-world economic adjustment. Instead, the updated GPR index treats both phases as components of an adverse event lifecycle, each warranting measurement not for what they reveal about cognitive states, but for what they *do* to economic behavior.

Critically, this approach avoids the pitfall of conflating uncertainty resolution with risk reduction. The collapse of uncertainty upon event realization does not imply safety; it often marks the onset of tangible consequences. By counting events rather than inferring mental states, the index remains anchored in observable phenomena, enhancing its reliability as a tool for tracking geopolitical strain. In this light, the updated GPR series is best understood not as a barometer of market anxiety or clarity, but as a systematic adverse event counter — one whose value lies in its ability to signal when geopolitics has moved from discourse to disruption.

## Misinterpreting the GPR Index as an Uncertainty Metric Risks Misguided Policy and Analysis

I want to be direct about what happens when we mistake an event counter for an uncertainty gauge. The consequences are not academic — they show up in policy briefs, central bank models, and risk management frameworks that treat a falling GPR reading as evidence that "uncertainty has resolved" and the coast is clear.

The research is unambiguous: threats and acts operate through different channels. Threats generate open-ended catastrophic scenarios that broadly suppress investment and consumption. Acts narrow the scenario space but trigger concrete disruptions — supply chain breaks, financial contagion, commodity shocks. Both are economically adverse. Both register as elevated GPR readings. Neither signals "uncertainty resolution" in any welfare-improving sense.

Yet I routinely see the index invoked as a proxy for geopolitical uncertainty — as if a spike means "markets are confused" and a decline means "clarity has returned." This is backwards. A decline often means the worst case has *materialized*. The invasion happened. The sanctions hit. The strait closed. Uncertainty didn't resolve; it *collapsed into reality*.

When policymakers feed this misreading into models, they get the sign wrong. They interpret post-event GPR moderation as a green light for investment, missing that the adverse consequences are just beginning to propagate. They calibrate "uncertainty shocks" using an index that measures event intensity, not cognitive dispersion. They confuse the map with the territory.

The GPR index is a superb adverse event counter. It is not, and was never designed to be, an uncertainty thermometer. Using it as one doesn't just add noise — it systematically inverts the signal at the moments that matter most. The remedy is simple: name the object correctly. Measure events with the GPR. Measure uncertainty with dispersion indices, forecast disagreement, option-implied volatility, or survey-based ambiguity gauges. Then ask how the two relate. That is the research agenda the index actually supports.

## Future Research Should Leverage the GPR Index’s Event-Centric Design for Targeted Insights

The GPR index’s strength lies in what it actually counts: observable adverse events, not inferred cognitive states. That clarity is a methodological asset — if we use it as intended.

Future work should pair the index with event-study designs that isolate economic responses to specific threat and act categories. The index’s newspaper-based construction allows disaggregation by event type (military threats, terrorist acts, diplomatic ruptures), geography, and intensity. Researchers can exploit this granularity to trace how distinct event classes propagate through trade, investment, and financial channels — without conflating them under a single "uncertainty" label.

A productive agenda would also cross-validate GPR spikes against independent event datasets (ICEWS, GDELT, conflict databases) to confirm signal fidelity and identify blind spots. Simultaneously, scholars should model the interaction between GPR-measured events and genuine uncertainty metrics — forecast dispersion, option-implied volatility, survey disagreement — to map how adverse events *generate* uncertainty rather than resolve it. The Caldara–Iacoviello framework already shows threats amplify uncertainty more than acts; the next step is quantifying that dynamic across horizons and sectors.

Policymakers benefit most when the index flags *when* geopolitics becomes materially disruptive, not when it becomes "uncertain." Central banks and finance ministries should integrate GPR event counts into scenario analysis as adverse shock triggers, while tracking uncertainty through dedicated instruments. The index was built to signal the arrival of trouble. Let it do that job — and build the complementary toolkit for everything else.
