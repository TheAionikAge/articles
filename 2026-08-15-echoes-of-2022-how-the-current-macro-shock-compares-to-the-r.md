## Two Supply Shocks, One Familiar Pattern

In February 2022, Russia's invasion of Ukraine detonated across global energy and commodity markets almost overnight. European gas prices quadrupled within weeks. Oil breached $120. Fertilizer, wheat, and a handful of industrial inputs — helium among them — went into acute shortage as sanctions bit and logistics rewired themselves in real time. The shock landed on an economy already straining under post-COVID demand and supply-chain knots, and central banks responded with the most aggressive tightening cycle in a generation.

Four years later, the script looks eerily familiar. A Middle East conflict has once again disrupted flows through the Strait of Hormuz, sustained for more than fifteen weeks. Oil and gas have surged. Fertilizer and shipping costs are climbing in sympathy. Analysts at the IMF, the ECB, and J.P. Morgan are, again, reaching for the word *stagflation*. The rhyme is loud enough that even cautious forecasters are flagging 2022 as the baseline scenario.

But the spiral is not a circle. The 2026 shock arrives at a fundamentally different radius: interest rates are already elevated, European energy dependence on any single hostile supplier has been deliberately reduced, and the post-2022 disinflation, however incomplete, has reset inflation expectations. Add a fiscal-AI complex that did not exist in 2022, and the transmission channels look different even where the trigger rhymes.

This is the comparison I want to walk through. Same mechanism, different starting conditions, different policy posture, different structural resilience. The question is not whether history is repeating — it plainly is not. The question is how much of the 2022 playbook still applies, and where the playbook itself has to be rewritten.

## The 2022 Shock: Anatomy of a Post-Pandemic Supply Crisis

The 2022 inflation surge was not a single event but a compound fracture — two fault lines crossing at once. The first was the post-pandemic demand recovery, still working through supply chains that had been bent by two years of COVID disruption. The second was Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, which weaponized Europe's energy dependence almost overnight. Together they produced the worst inflation readings in four decades.

The numbers tell the story sharply. US headline CPI peaked at 9.1% in June 2022 — the highest since the early 1980s. The euro area crossed 10% in several member states, driven by natural gas prices that at one point rose more than tenfold relative to pre-crisis baselines. Research from the Bank of Finland estimates that geopolitical risk alone added roughly two percentage points to euro-area inflation in 2022, while shaving up to one percentage point from GDP growth. That is the magnitude of the impulse we are now using as a baseline.

The transmission mechanism mattered as much as the size. This was not a demand-pull episode; it was a supply shock delivered through an energy channel that fed directly into food, fertilizer, transport, and manufacturing inputs. European economies — heavily reliant on Russian pipeline gas — absorbed the largest direct hit. The United States, less energy-dependent on Russia but still globally exposed via oil and commodity markets, caught the inflation through import prices and second-round wage effects as labor markets remained tight.

The policy response was aggressive and broadly synchronized. The Federal Reserve raised rates 75 basis points at four consecutive meetings. The ECB ended negative-rate policy and began its own hiking cycle later that summer. Both institutions explicitly framed the response as restoring price anchoring credibility after the 2021 "transitory" misjudgment.

What I want to fix in the reader's mind is the *structure* of that episode: a geopolitical surprise producing a concentrated commodity supply shock, layered onto an already-stretched post-pandemic economy, transmitted primarily through energy prices, met with delayed but forceful monetary tightening, and resolved only after demand destruction and supply substitution did the work that policy alone could not. When I look at the 2026 Middle East-driven shock, this is the rhyme class I am comparing against — same chord progression, but the instrument has been retuned. The next section will name what is genuinely new at this radius.

## The 2026 Shock: A Reignited Energy and Commodity Disruption

The current episode rhymes with 2022 at the structural level but lands on a different chord. The trigger is a Middle East confrontation — centered on Iran — that has disrupted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz for more than fifteen weeks. Roughly a fifth of global oil and a significant share of liquefied natural gas transits that chokepoint, so even partial closure produces immediate price effects. But the disruption extends well beyond crude. Helium supplies, critical for semiconductor manufacturing and medical imaging, have tightened sharply because Qatar's export infrastructure sits on the same shipping lane. Fertilizer shipments — urea and ammonia in particular — have been rerouted or delayed, reviving the agricultural-input channel that drove 2022's food-price surge.

The price action has been unmistakable. Brent has pushed back toward triple-digit territory, European TTF gas benchmarks have roughly doubled off their 2025 trough, and shipping insurance premia for Hormuz transits have spiked. The IMF's April 2026 World Economic Outlook and the UN's mid-2026 *World Economic Situation and Prospects* both flag a renewed commodity supply shock as the dominant near-term inflation driver, with JPMorgan's economic trends note corroborating the energy-led reacceleration.

What matters for the inflation transmission is the second-round footprint. Developed-economy headline inflation is now running around 2.9%, up from roughly 2.6% in 2025 — a modest absolute move but a directional reversal after two years of disinflation. The sharper signal is in US core PCE, which the IMF has revised up to 3.4% from a prior 2.9% projection. Core matters because it tells us whether the energy impulse is leaking into wages, services, and goods prices broadly, or staying contained in the fuel line. A 50-basis-point upward revision in core is not 2022's 9.1% headline, but it is enough to break the disinflation narrative central banks had been carefully constructing.

The emerging-market picture is more stressed. Importers without domestic energy buffers are absorbing the shock through current-account deterioration and currency depreciation, which feeds back into imported inflation. That is the same feedback loop that amplified 2022 in Turkey, parts of Latin America, and South Asia.

What I want to register here is the *mechanism*: a geopolitical disruption producing a concentrated commodity supply shock, transmitted through energy and downstream inputs, reaccelerating headline inflation and pushing core forecasts higher. The chord progression is familiar. The next sections will examine why the instrument — starting conditions, policy posture, structural resilience — sounds different this time.

## Where the Episodes Diverge: Starting Conditions and Resilience

The mechanism rhymes. The instrument does not. Four structural differences separate the 2026 shock from its 2022 predecessor, and they explain why the current episode reads as a muted chord rather than a full replay.

First, Europe's energy dependency has been redrawn. In 2022, the European Union imported roughly 40% of its natural gas from Russian pipelines, leaving the continent acutely exposed when those flows were sanctioned and weaponized. By 2026, that share has collapsed. Renewables have scaled, LNG import terminals have diversified supply toward the United States, Qatar, and West Africa, and efficiency gains from the 2022–23 demand-destruction episode have stuck. The European Commission's Spring 2026 forecast explicitly notes this reduced fossil-fuel reliance as a buffer against the Hormuz disruption. The continent is not insulated — TTF gas has still roughly doubled off its 2025 trough — but the transmission coefficient is smaller. A doubling from a low base hurts less than a doubling from a position of strategic vulnerability.

Second, supply chains have matured out of their post-pandemic fragility. The 2021–22 inflation surge was amplified by container shipping bottlenecks, semiconductor shortages, and just-in-time inventory systems that had no slack. Those bottlenecks have been resolved, inventory-to-sales ratios have normalized, and shipping costs remain near pre-pandemic baselines outside the Hormuz corridor. The supply side enters this shock with elasticity it lacked four years ago.

Third, AI-related capital expenditure is providing a partial demand offset. The compute-phase transition — training capex giving way to inference deployment at scale — is sustaining investment growth even as energy costs bite. Nvidia's revenue mix has shifted toward inference, reflecting a broader pattern: capital formation is following energy density into the AI infrastructure stack, which crowds in private demand even as consumer real incomes are squeezed.

Fourth, the demand backdrop is fundamentally different. 2022's shock landed on an economy still running hot from pandemic stimulus — excess savings, labor shortages, and a reopening demand surge. 2026 arrives into a mature cycle with labor markets closer to balance, fiscal stimulus largely withdrawn, and household balance sheets more cautious. The acute demand impulse that turned 2022's supply shock into a wage-price spiral is absent.

The rhyme class is the same: geopolitical disruption, energy supply shock, inflation reacceleration. But the radius has changed — lower dependency, more elastic supply, an AI-driven investment offset, and a cooler demand backdrop. That is why the IMF and UN describe the current episode as a slowdown-with-inflation risk rather than a stagflation crisis. The spiral turns, but on different terrain.

## Policy Response: Hiking Into a Shock vs. Holding at the Peak

The most revealing contrast between 2022 and 2026 is not in the inflation data — it is in what central banks are doing about it. The two episodes have met opposite policy postures, and that divergence tells you almost everything about why the current shock feels muted rather than replayed.

In 2022, the Federal Reserve and its peers were hiking *into* the shock. The Fed moved from near-zero to a 5.25–5.50% funds rate over sixteen months, the fastest tightening cycle since the early 1980s. The European Central Bank walked an even tighter path from negative rates into restrictive territory. The logic was straightforward: a supply shock that risks de-anchoring inflation expectations demands a forceful demand-side response, even at the cost of growth. Central banks accepted the recession risk as the price of credibility.

In 2026, the Fed is holding at 3.50–3.75% — already restrictive, but well below the 2022 terminal — while markets oscillate between pricing further hikes and the cuts that were on the table before the Hormuz disruption. Long-end yields sit elevated at roughly 4.2–4.7%, reflecting term premium and inflation risk compensation rather than an active hiking campaign. The ECB faces a similar bind: energy-driven headline pressure against an already-weak growth backdrop that limits room to tighten.

This is not dovishness. It is *constraint*. The starting point matters. In 2022, policy entered the shock with massive accommodation still in the system; the hiking cycle had room to run because rates had so far to rise. In 2026, policy enters restrictive, with balance sheets still larger than pre-pandemic norms and the memory of the 2022–23 demand destruction still fresh. The Fed's 9–3 dissent fracture on direction — hike versus hold — is itself a signal: the committee cannot agree on whether the shock warrants more tightening or patience, because the cost of being wrong in either direction is unusually high.

The spiral rhymes in posture: central banks face the same stagflationary trade-off, the same credibility-versus-growth calculus. But the radius has changed. Hiking into a supply shock from a position of accommodation is one thing. Holding the peak while a new energy impulse arrives is another. The latter is the harder, and more honest, problem — and it is why the 2026 response looks less like aggression and more like a high-wire act.

## Rhyme, Not Repeat: What the Comparison Tells Us

The spiral turns, and the pattern is recognizable. A geopolitical surprise severs an energy artery. Prices spike. Inflation reaccelerates. Growth softens. Central banks face the same stagflationary trade-off they faced four years ago. This is the rhyme class: a supply-side shock transmitted through commodity channels into headline inflation, met with monetary constraint and fiscal anxiety.

But the radius has changed, and the radius is the whole story.

In 2022, the shock arrived at a moment of maximum vulnerability — post-pandemic demand at full boil, European energy dependence on Russia near total, supply chains still knotted from COVID, and policy rates pinned at the zero lower bound. The Fed and ECB had to hike aggressively precisely because they had so far to climb. The 9.1% US CPI peak and double-digit euro-area inflation were the price of starting from that position.

In 2026, the same transmission mechanism hits a fundamentally different economy. European gas storage sits near full. Renewable capacity has displaced roughly a third of pre-war Russian fossil demand. Supply chains have regained elasticity. AI-related capital expenditure — data centers, grid buildout, semiconductor fabrication — is providing a demand offset that did not exist in 2022. The Fed enters at 3.50–3.75%, already restrictive; the ECB enters with an inflation print near target rather than far above it. The shock is real, but the denominator is larger and the starting leverage is lower.

That is why headline inflation lands at ~2.9% rather than 9%, and why core PCE forecasts at 3.4% reflect an impulse, not a regime change.

The downside risks remain. A prolonged Hormuz disruption — beyond fifteen weeks — would test the resilience I have just described. Helium and fertilizer constraints feed into manufacturing and food prices with lags that have not yet shown up in the data. And the Fed's 9–3 dissent fracture signals genuine uncertainty about whether the shock warrants further tightening or patience. If the conflict extends, if term premia widen further, if the AI capex offset fades — the rhyme could deepen into something uglier.

For now, the lesson is restraint in interpretation. The mechanism rhymes. The magnitude does not. This is a muted crisis wearing familiar clothes, and the appropriate policy posture is to recognize the pattern without confusing it for the prior instance. The spiral turns; we have seen this turn before. What we do at this radius is the variable that matters.
