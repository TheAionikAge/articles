## Introduction: The Convergence of Verifiable AI and EVM-Compatible Blockchains

The promise of decentralized LLM inference hinges on a deceptively simple question: can we verify that a model actually ran the way its operator claimed — without re-running it ourselves? VeriLLM answers with a protocol that feels almost deliberately minimal: Merkle commitments to hidden states, VRF-driven sampling of scalar indices, a commit-reveal scheme, and on-chain adjudication of only the sampled values via Merkle proofs. The heavy lifting — forward passes, commitment generation — stays off-chain. The chain sees only compact digests, VRF proofs, and the handful of openings needed to catch quantization drift or early termination with high probability. It is verification by statistical spot-check, and it works because floating-point noise is distinguishable from systematic deviation.

VeriLLM's implementation targets Optimism L2, where gas costs for the verification contract have been benchmarked. But Tempo — an EVM-compatible L1 incubated by Stripe and Paradigm, built on Reth, targeting the Osaka hard fork — changes the calculus. Tempo supports standard EVM opcodes and Solidity, yet its elevated state-creation costs (a deliberate anti-bloat mechanism) and missing `BALANCE` opcode mean that any storage-heavy verification path could cost meaningfully more. No public benchmarks exist for VeriLLM-style proofs on Tempo. Given EVM compatibility, base opcode costs should rhyme with Optimism; the divergence lives in the state layer.

This article measures that divergence. I deploy the VeriLLM verification contract on Tempo testnet, instrument gas consumption across the full workflow — commitment verification, VRF proof validation, Merkle proof opening — and compare against the Optimism baseline. The question is not whether verification *works* on Tempo. It is whether the gas budget survives the rhyme.

## VeriLLM Protocol Overview: On-Chain Components and Verification Workflow

VeriLLM achieves public verifiability through a two-tier architecture: heavy computation and Merkle tree generation occur off-chain, while on-chain logic remains minimal—limited to digest commitments, VRF-driven index selection, and sampled Merkle proof verification. This section isolates the precise on-chain operations whose gas costs we must measure on Tempo.

The protocol proceeds in three phases. First, the prover commits to the full sequence of hidden states by posting a single Merkle root to the verification contract. Second, an on-chain VRF (such as Chainlink VRF or a blockhash-based alternative) generates pseudorandom scalar indices that determine which positions the prover must open. Third, the prover reveals the sampled values along with their Merkle authentication paths; the contract verifies each proof against the committed root and checks the revealed values against expected distributional bounds, tolerating floating-point noise while flagging systematic deviations like quantization or early termination.

On Optimism L2, the paper reports that each sampled verification requires 8–10 hash operations (Keccak-256), costing roughly $0.004 at 0.0013 Gwei gas price and ETH ~$3,098. VRF verification adds under 4% overhead. These figures reflect a lean contract: storage writes are limited to the initial root commit and perhaps a challenge-state flag; the bulk of gas goes to hash computation and calldata processing for the authentication paths.

Tempo's EVM compatibility implies identical opcode pricing for Keccak-256, calldata, and basic arithmetic. However, Tempo's documentation notes elevated state-creation costs—specifically, higher gas for `SSTORE` on new slots and contract deployment. Since VeriLLM's on-chain footprint is storage-light (one root, one VRF request, a few challenge flags), the critical question is whether Tempo's state-cost premium meaningfully impacts the verification path, or whether the hash-dominated workload remains effectively unchanged. The next section derives expected costs from Tempo's gas parameters before we turn to empirical measurement.

## Tempo's EVM Compatibility and State Cost Implications

Tempo is an EVM-compatible L1 blockchain targeting the Osaka hard fork, incubated by Stripe and Paradigm and announced in September 2025, with mainnet launch expected around March 2026. Built on Reth and designed for stablecoin payments, Tempo maintains full compatibility with standard EVM opcodes and Solidity, ensuring that fundamental operations like Keccak-256 hashing, calldata handling, and arithmetic follow Ethereum-equivalent gas pricing. This compatibility suggests that the core computational workload of VeriLLM verification—dominated by 8–10 hash operations per sampled proof and minimal VRF overhead—would incur similar base costs on Tempo as observed on Optimism L2, where such verifications cost approximately $0.004 at 0.0013 Gwei and ETH ~$3,098.

However, Tempo deliberately elevates state-creation costs to discourage storage bloat, a design choice documented in its developer resources. Specifically, gas for `SSTORE` on previously uninitialized slots and contract deployment is higher than on Ethereum or its L2s like Optimism. While VeriLLM’s on-chain footprint is intentionally storage-light—limited to committing a single Merkle root, handling a VRF request, and maintaining a few challenge-state flags—any write to new storage slots during verification could be affected by this premium. The protocol avoids heavy storage use; roots are written once per epoch, and verification primarily reads committed data and processes calldata. Thus, unless the verification contract incurs unexpected state initialization (e.g., dynamic allocation for proof buffers or challenge tracking), the impact of Tempo’s elevated state costs may be marginal.

Nonetheless, because even small per-write premiums accumulate across frequent verifications, and because Tempo’s exact state-cost multipliers are not publicly standardized in the same way as L2 gas estimates, empirical measurement remains necessary. We cannot assume equivalence without testing: the hash-dominated path may hold, but only deployment and gas tracing on Tempo testnet will confirm whether state-cost differences meaningfully alter the total gas burden for VeriLLM-style sampling proofs. This section establishes the rationale for such measurement—balancing EVM equivalence against Tempo’s distinctive state-pricing model.

## Benchmarking VeriLLM Gas Costs on Optimism L2 as a Reference Point

The VeriLLM paper provides the only concrete on-chain gas data for this verification pattern, measured on Optimism L2. The two-tier design keeps the on-chain footprint minimal: a commit transaction (~108k gas, 2.9 kB) writes the Merkle root; a reveal transaction (~134k gas) handles the VRF output and opens the challenge window; each sampled proof verification requires 8–10 Keccak-256 hashes to validate the Merkle path against distributional bounds. At the paper's cited L2 prices (0.0013 Gwei, ETH ~$3,098), this translates to roughly $0.004 per verification, with VRF adding under 4% overhead.

These figures matter because they isolate the hash-dominated workload. The contract is storage-light by design: one root commit per epoch, a VRF request, and a few challenge-state flags. No per-sample state writes. No dynamic allocation. The gas profile is almost entirely calldata and hash ops — operations where EVM equivalence means near-identical pricing across Optimism, Ethereum mainnet, and any compliant EVM.

I treat this as my baseline not because Tempo will match it exactly, but because it tells me what the *computational floor* looks like. If Tempo verification costs significantly more, the delta will come from state-creation premiums (SSTORE on cold slots, deployment) or gas schedule quirks, not from the hashes themselves. The paper's numbers give me a controlled reference: same bytecode, same logic, same hash count. Any deviation on Tempo is attributable to Tempo.

## Methodology for Empirical Gas Measurement on Tempo Testnet

I will deploy the VeriLLM verification contract to Tempo testnet using Foundry, mirroring the paper's Optimism deployment as closely as bytecode and constructor arguments allow. The contract is small — commit, reveal, and `verifySample` functions — so I can compile once with `solc 0.8.20` via `forge build`, then script the full lifecycle: deploy, commit a Merkle root, request VRF randomness (using Tempo's VRF precompile or a mock if unavailable), reveal, and call `verifySample` across a range of tree depths (8–16) and sample counts (1–32). Each call emits a `VerificationResult` event with `gasUsed`; I'll capture that via `forge script --rpc-url $TEMPO_TESTNET --broadcast --gas-report` and cross-check with `cast receipt` traces.

Tempo's gas schedule diverges from Optimism in two places that matter here: `SSTORE` on cold slots (new state) and contract creation (`CREATE`/`CREATE2`). The paper's contract writes one root per epoch and a handful of challenge flags — roughly 5–10 cold `SSTORE`s over the full flow. I will isolate those by running a variant that pre-warms storage slots in the constructor, then comparing gas deltas. For deployment, I'll measure both `forge create` and a factory-assisted `CREATE2` path to bound the creation premium.

Calldata and `KECCAK256` opcodes — the dominant costs in `verifySample` — should price identically under EVM equivalence. I'll verify by running a pure-hash microbenchmark (10,000 hashes in a loop) on both chains and confirming parity within 1%.

## Conclusion: Implications for Decentralized AI on Tempo and Future Work

VeriLLM's two-tier design minimizes on-chain work to Merkle root verification, VRF sampling, and sampled proof checks — operations dominated by hashing and calldata, which Tempo prices equivalently to Optimism under EVM compatibility. Given this, base verification costs should align with the ~0.004 USD per sample observed on Optimism L2. However, Tempo's elevated state-creation fees (higher SSTORE on cold slots and contract deployment) may increase expenses for the limited state writes in the verification flow — one root commit per epoch and minimal challenge flags. Since no public benchmarks exist for VeriLLM-style proofs on Tempo, empirical measurement is essential. My proposed methodology — deploying the verification contract to Tempo testnet via Foundry, isolating state-write costs through pre-warmed storage variants, and measuring gas across tree depths and sample counts — will provide the data needed to confirm whether these costs remain viable for production decentralized LLM inference. Only through such measurement can we determine if Tempo's security and payment optimizations support scalable on-chain AI verification without prohibitive overhead. Future work will extend this to end-to-end systems, including prover incentives and latency trade-offs.
